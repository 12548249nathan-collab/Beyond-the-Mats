BEYOND THE MATS

The mat is just the beginning.

A free, volunteer-run Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu program designed to build the emotional and social skills young people carry with them off the mat.

Why this program exists

Youth martial arts programs routinely claim to build character, confidence, discipline, respect, and resilience. These words appear on gym walls and in marketing copy across the industry, often with little explanation of how a given program actually produces them.

Research suggests that outcomes depend less on the art itself than on the culture surrounding its instruction. A program's effect on a young person seems to hinge on how it is designed and taught, not merely on which techniques are practiced. If culture, not curriculum, is the active ingredient, then program design should be treated as seriously as technical instruction.

Beyond the Mats treats the claim that martial arts build character as a design problem rather than a slogan.

Why Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu

Unlike a martial art practiced against a compliant partner or no partner at all, BJJ cannot be trained without another person who is actively providing resistance and feedback. A student cannot progress in the art while ignoring their partner's experience, communication, or safety.

That structural feature, present in every single class, is the foundation this program is designed around.

What we build

The program uses the five core competencies defined by the Collaborative for Academic, Social, and Emotional Learning (CASEL) to describe what social-emotional growth looks like in practice.

Self-awareness

Every practitioner has a distinct set of strengths and limitations. Progress in BJJ depends on identifying them accurately and training accordingly. A student who doesn't know their own game cannot improve it.

Self-management

Safe training depends on students controlling their actions under the stress of live resistance, and on managing ego and frustration. No single class produces visible improvement; consistency over months does.

Social awareness

Every round puts a student in direct negotiation with another person's comfort, skill level, and limits. Training with a wide range of partners builds the habit of reading people who are not like you.

Relationship skills

BJJ requires a level of trust that is unusual for a competitive activity. Coaches provide instruction, but the person a student learns the most from, day to day, is whoever they are training with.

Responsible decision-making

Students constantly choose between communicating honestly or pushing through unsafely, and between releasing a submission when a partner taps or chasing a "win." These moments repeat so often that they function as genuine practice rather than a single test.

How classes work

Skills are built through two pathways. Some are learned implicitly, simply by doing the activity through cooperative, partner-based drilling. Others are taught explicitly, named and discussed. The program is designed to use both deliberately rather than defaulting to one.

Every class follows the same basic shape: a warm-up, a handfighting or positional game, a longer block of live, objective-driven practice, and a closing discussion. That discussion is not a generic "how was class" check-in. Questions are written in advance for each class and designed to connect what happened physically on the mat to something a student can carry with them off of it.

The curriculum is organized into four sequential blocks, each pairing a grappling objective with a developmental one:

  1. Positional movement, alongside a shared vocabulary for consent and violence
  2. Positional security, alongside safety: protecting oneself and supporting a partner
  3. Submissions, alongside community building and social trust
  4. Escaping bad positions, alongside resilience and how students respond to failure

Programs are free for families. Parents complete short, anonymous check-ins over the course of the program so we can track what is working and where there is room to improve.

A note from the founder

I was enrolled in martial arts classes at the age of five at the request of my elementary school teachers. They had seen that a local program, where instructors took a real interest in supporting students in all areas of their lives, helped young students adjust to the structure of school. That program was incredibly influential in my life. The community, culture, and sense of belonging grounded me and inspired me to pursue goals I once viewed as unattainable.

I later became an instructor and eventually the lead black belt instructor, responsible for developing and running new programs, including adult and youth BJJ programs. During that time, I was also privileged to be sponsored by the U.S. Army National Sport Karate Team to compete nationally. While traveling for competition and training, I noticed that not every martial arts school produced and modeled the virtues I had come to associate so closely with the martial arts.

That led me to the questions behind this program: How do martial arts programs promote emotional and social development rather than just athletic skill? How are those cultures designed, and how can they be replicated?

Nathan McAllister, Founder

An honest starting point

This program is an effort to learn about the potential impact of BJJ programs that are grounded in research and designed intentionally. It is not a research study, and any patterns that emerge should be read as observations worth paying attention to, not findings.

The goal is not a finished model, but a well-documented, honest starting point, one built carefully enough to be worth learning from, whether or not every assumption behind it proves correct.
